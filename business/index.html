What it actually teaches

Not how to go viral. A video for a business that people can walk into has one job: make somebody decide to come in. Two hundred views from people who live twenty minutes away are worth more than twenty thousand scattered across the country, because none of those twenty thousand can walk in.

Sixteen lessons, four parts, every one of them with something to do at the end rather than something to watch.

  • What the video is for. The only three videos you ever need, why the first second decides everything, and why you already own all the equipment required.
  • Filming it. Light, which is the whole game and is free. Sound, which is why people leave. Holding the phone still, close and the right way up. And how to film in a room full of your own customers without feeling ridiculous.
  • Making it watchable. Cutting it far shorter than feels right, captions that do not disappear behind the app's own buttons, the music rule that quietly mutes business accounts, and what belongs in the caption instead of the video.
  • Keeping it going. How to film a month's worth of your own videos in one afternoon, a posting plan you can hand to a member of staff, and what to do the first time one of them flops.

What this is not

  • Not a course about going viral. Nothing in here is about chasing a trend or doing a dance. If a video of yours is seen by four hundred people in your own town and three of them come in, it worked, and that is the standard the whole course is written to.
  • Not a live course. No calls, no group chat, no Tuesday at seven. It sits there and you do it when the shop is quiet.
  • Not a shopping list. You are not going to be told to buy a ring light, a gimbal or a microphone. The single biggest improvement in the whole course is wiping your lens, and that is free.
  • Not done in a week. The honest bit: this only pays off if you are still posting in three months. A lot of the course is about making that survivable rather than about making one good video.

Common questions

Do I need a camera?

No. A phone from the last five years, a window that gets daylight, and ten quiet minutes. Phone cameras stopped being the limiting factor years ago. What actually separates a good video from a bad one is light, sound and how close the camera is, and all three of those are free.

Do I have to be on camera?

No. The course covers three kinds of video: the place, the thing you make, and the person. You can run a whole account on the first two and never appear. It is slower, but it works.

How long does it take?

Sixteen lessons, at your own pace, no fixed times. Most people work through it over a couple of weeks in the quiet hour of the day. Access lasts twelve months.

Is this the same as your creator certification?

No, and they do not overlap at all. The UGC Content Creator Certification teaches a freelancer how to get paid by brands: pricing, usage rights, contracts, chasing payment. This one teaches a business to film its own front door. Different job, different person.
